    05 · The company

    What it earns

    Highly interactive sites do pay, in specific ways. They win attention in the first fraction of a second: people judge a page's visual appeal in about 50 milliseconds, and those snap judgements agree closely with what they decide after a longer look (Lindgaard et al., 2006). Appeal then leaks into everything else. In a classic ATM study, the better-looking interface was rated easier to use, even after people had used it and found it no easier (Tractinsky et al., 2000).

    Play also holds people. Marketing researchers modelled the "flow" state online and found that interactivity and the feeling of being somewhere feed it, and flow relates to more browsing and shopping (Novak, Hoffman & Yung, 2000). Letting people build or configure something makes them value it more (Norton, Mochon & Ariely, 2012).

    For a studio, the site is the sales pitch. Lusion's stated aim for its own award-winning site was to "showcase our digital craftsmanship" and "engage potential brand and agency partners". Bruno Simon's drivable 3D portfolio drew more than 400,000 visitors and won Awwwards Site of the Year in 2020. For a store, the numbers that get quoted come mostly from the companies selling the technology. The best independent study found that letting shoppers try products in augmented reality raised sales mainly where they were unsure, for unfamiliar brands and expensive items (Tan, Chandukala & Reddy, 2022). Interactivity paid when it answered a question.

    06 · The bill

    What it costs the company

    Every effect has weight, and weight costs sales. The cleanest causal number in this whole study is Vodafone's: the same landing page, half the traffic each, one version 31% faster to show its main content. The fast one sold 8% more (web.dev, 2021). Google's earlier research found that 53% of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than three seconds, and that going from one second to three raises the probability of a bounce by 32%.

    Pages keep getting heavier anyway. The median mobile page shipped 558 KB of JavaScript in 2024, up 14% in a year, and almost half of it was never used (HTTP Archive, 2024). Award-style sites sit far above the median. So does this one.

    The subtler cost is memory. When Penn State researchers gave an e-commerce site more and more interactive tools, people remembered more of what the tools showed and less of everything else, and they spent less total time on the most interactive version than on a moderate one (Xu & Sundar, 2016). That is the quiz you just took. Educational psychologists call the same pattern "seductive details": interesting extras that crowd out the point (Harp & Mayer, 1998).

    07 · The person

    What it takes from you

    Gloria Mark has timed how long people stay on one screen before switching for two decades. The averages keep falling.

    Some of that switching is designed. Infinite scroll removes the natural place to stop, and its inventor, Aza Raskin, has said publicly that he regrets what it became. A field study of 46 people found that scrolling runs in loops within loops, and that most of the reasons people finally stop come from their lives, not from the app (Rixen et al., 2023). Autoplay works the same way. When 76 heavy Netflix users turned it off, their sessions got about 18 minutes shorter (Schaffner & Chetty, 2025).

    Underneath is a reward system that responds most to surprises. Dopamine signalling spikes for unexpected rewards, and the response shifts to whatever predicts them: the pull, the badge, the buzz (Haynes, 2018). And people are poor judges of their own use. A meta-analysis of 106 comparisons found that under 10% of self-reports landed within 5% of logged reality (Parry et al., 2021).

    Motion has a physical cost too. In a national balance test, 35% of US adults over 40 showed signs of vestibular dysfunction (Agrawal et al., 2009), and parallax and large motion on screen can trigger dizziness and nausea in people with vestibular disorders. The web standard for it is WCAG 2.3.3, which asks that motion triggered by interaction can be turned off. That is what the Calm mode button at the top of this page does, and it switches on by itself if your device asks for reduced motion.

    A caution on one famous study: the claim that a phone on your desk drains your thinking even when it is off (Ward et al., 2017) failed a preregistered replication (Ruiz Pardo & Minda, 2022). Not every scary statistic survives.

    08 · Synthesis

    Where the curve bends

    Put the findings together and a shape appears. The first bits of interactivity earn a lot: attention, trust, memory for the thing being shown. Each extra bit earns less, while its costs (weight, distraction, motion, time the user did not mean to spend) keep climbing. Drag the slider to move along it.

    Interactivity pays when it carries the message and costs when it competes with it. Past that point, the same techniques spend the user's time, attention and comfort to buy numbers, like time on site, that do not reliably turn into profit.

    09 · What to do with it

    Interactivity, on purpose

    1. Make the interaction carry the content. If people only remember what they touch, put the point in the thing they touch.
    2. Spend a speed budget. Every effect has to earn its kilobytes. Load the shell first and the toy second.
    3. Honour reduced motion. It is one media query, and a third of older adults may need it.
    4. Give back the stopping cue. An end to the feed, a pause before the next episode.
    5. Measure outcomes, not minutes. Time on site is a cost to the person and only sometimes revenue to the company.
